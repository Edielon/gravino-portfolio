Norbert Building Retrofit, Cor Jesu College

Digos City, Philippines · 2021 to 2023

Cor Jesu College’s six-storey Norbert Building was left unsafe by a 6.3-magnitude earthquake. As project engineer and project management coordinator, I compared the building before and after the retrofit design, costed every defect the demolition uncovered, redesigned the foundation around a property-line problem, drew the shop drawings the crews built from, and reported cost and progress every week.

  • 6storeys retrofitted
  • 1.6 kmfrom the Digos Fault
  • 0.915 → 0.675 sfirst-mode period, before and after
  • 107 tof steel dampers

A building that twisted

The Digos Fault runs 1.6 km from the campus. The national hazard map rates the site prone to ground shaking and susceptible to liquefaction.

The ETABS model of the existing building showed why it had failed. A sound building sways back and forth in its first mode. This one swayed and twisted at the same time, and it was too flexible, with a first-mode period of 0.915 seconds.

Steel frames that stop the twist

The retrofit added steel dampers, ladder-shaped Vierendeel truss frames fixed to the existing columns from the ground floor to the sixth, and a new steel frame for the stairs. I compared the two models mode by mode.

Top view of the first three vibration modes from ETABS. Left: the existing building. Right: with the steel dampers and stair frame. Movement is exaggerated so it can be seen.

Every period dropped by a quarter to a third, so the building is much stiffer, and the first mode became a clean sway instead of a twist.

0.915 → 0.675 sfirst-mode period, before and after the retrofit

What the demolition uncovered

Aftershocks kept the planning team from inspecting the whole building beforehand. Opening it up showed why it had performed so badly:

  • Slab bars 160 mm apart instead of 120 mm, and top and bottom bars 15 mm too close because no spacers were used.
  • Bars bent up at a quarter of the span instead of a third.
  • Concrete testing under 3,000 psi.
  • A hollow beam on the sixth floor, and beams buckled in and out so far that bolts would not line up.

I photographed every defect and costed each fix. The structural engineers for the retrofit and for the previous contractor reviewed them together on site, and the previous contractor covered the rectification.

A foundation that fits the site

The design added footings on both sides of the building. On site, two of them ran into the property line, where they would have damaged a fence and a septic tank. I sent the structural engineer photos and proposed combining two footings into one.

The revised plan used three combined footings: CF-1 at 8.0 × 4.2 m, CF-2 at 17.0 × 6.0 m and CF-3 at 6.775 × 4.8 m, each 0.7 m thick in 28 MPa concrete with 20 mm bars.

Excavation then hit a high water table and the soil began to erode. Engineered fill was costly and unreliable in wet ground, so we protected the soil, shored the pit with cut I-beams and dug down to firm soil. I re-estimated the rebar for the real conditions.

41.8 tof foundation rebar for the actual conditions, up from 22.7 t

Drawn first, built second

I drew shop drawings for everything the crews built: the damper frames, their connections, beam jacketing, the elevator shaft and the stairs. Drawing in SketchUp meant the manager, the engineers and the college could see a site problem without being on site, and approve a fix from the drawing.

Cost and schedule, every week

Every weekly meeting got the same report from me: the Gantt chart and what was late and why, the material inventory, and actual against allotted cost for labour and materials. It let us see overruns coming and adjust in time.

The steel was prefabricated in Vietnam. I tracked the shipment and inspected it with the structural engineer when it arrived, alongside concrete compression and steel tensile tests.

Prices rose through the COVID period, so I tracked escalation item by item, and negotiated where I could. A PHP 50 per m³ discount on ready-mix concrete saved PHP 65,300.

PHP 65,300saved on ready-mix concrete through a negotiated discount

Keeping the college in the loop

Shop drawings went through the weekly project management meeting, then to the structural engineer, then to the college president for final approval before I requested the materials. When the board of directors visited, the project manager and I presented the finished Vierendeel truss installation.
